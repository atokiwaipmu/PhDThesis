This thesis examines how certain large-scale effects in simulations, known as super-sample covariance (SSC), and other subtle issues influence the accuracy and interpretation of higher-order statistics from weak gravitational lensing data. The study begins with a review of modern cosmology, highlighting how weak lensing—by measuring small distortions in the shapes of distant galaxies caused by intervening matter—can help us understand dark matter, dark energy, and the overall structure of the Universe. It then focuses on "beyond-two-point" statistics, which can reveal non-Gaussian patterns in the matter distribution, thus potentially improving our ability to determine key cosmological parameters.

To achieve this, we employ a simulation-based framework utilizing two distinct $N$-body simulation strategies: the BIGBOX simulation set from the HalfDome project, featuring $6144^3$ particles in a $3750\, \mathrm{Mpc}/h$ box, and the TILED simulation set, composed of multiple $1024^3$-particle boxes each $625\, \mathrm{Mpc}/h$ in size. Both simulations share identical cosmological parameters but differ in volume coverage and box replication schemes, providing a controlled environment to isolate super-sample modes and other effects. We project three-dimensional matter distributions into full-sky convergence maps at multiple redshifts under the Born approximation. These maps are then processed to mimic realistic observational conditions by incorporating shape noise and applying Gaussian smoothing. Using a Fibonacci grid to extract numerous $10^\circ \times 10^\circ$ patches, we measure a suite of lensing observables, including the angular power spectrum, bispectrum, probability distribution function (PDF), peak and minima counts, and Minkowski functionals. We compare each observable's mean values, variances, and covariance matrices between BIGBOX and TILED, and relate them to theoretical predictions from models like Halofit and hmpdf.

Our results show that the average measured statistics from BIGBOX and TILED agree at the $1\%$ level, confirming the baseline cosmological consistency of both sets. However, the variances and covariances differ substantially, by up to $10\%$-$30\%$ at high multipoles and redshifts. We attribute these differences to super-sample covariance, which arises from large-scale modes present in BIGBOX but missing in TILED's smaller effective volume. While shape noise and smoothing do influence certain statistics (notably the angular power spectrum and peak/minima counts), SSC remains the dominant contributor to the observed variance patterns. Moreover, we identify and characterize box replication artifacts, which introduce systematic biases and can mimic or mask SSC signatures in the data, particularly affecting the mean of some observables and distorting the distribution of high peaks and deep minima.

These findings highlight a few key points for future research and survey design. Including large-scale modes in simulations is crucial for realistic modeling of uncertainties. Careful treatment of shape noise and smoothing procedures is needed to maintain the reliability of the results. Mitigating artifacts from box replication is also important to avoid systematic errors. As upcoming weak lensing surveys like the Rubin Observatory's LSST, Euclid, and the Roman Space Telescope aim for very high precision, properly accounting for SSC and other subtleties will be essential. In the future, we plan to reduce these artifacts, consider different cosmological models and baryonic effects, and incorporate these improvements into survey planning and analysis pipelines. By refining both the simulation methods and the interpretation strategies, this thesis contributes to making weak lensing an even more powerful tool for understanding the fundamental properties of the Universe.
