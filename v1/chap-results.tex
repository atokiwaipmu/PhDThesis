This chapter presents a comprehensive comparison of weak lensing statistics derived from two distinct simulation sets: BIGBOX and TILED. The primary objective is to quantify the differences in mean values, covariance matrices, and correlation matrices between these simulations to assess the impacts of super-sample covariance and other systematic effects on the measurements. Additionally, the chapter investigates the influence of shape noise, varying smoothing scales, and box replication artifacts on the results. Unless otherwise specified, the main findings are based on noiseless simulations with a smoothing scale of 2 arcminutes. Furthermore, the power spectrum ($C_\ell$) and bispectrum results are normalized by factors of $\ell(\ell+1)/2\pi$ and $\ell^4$, respectively, to facilitate meaningful comparisons.

\section{Comparison of Mean and Variance for Summary Statistics}
In this section, we analyze and compare the mean ($\mu$) and variance ($\sigma^2$) of summary statistics derived from the BIGBOX and TILED simulations. Figures~\ref{fig:ell_main} and~\ref{fig:nu_main} illustrate these comparisons for the angular power spectrum, bispectrum, and other higher-order statistics, highlighting the differences arising from simulation methodologies and super-sample covariance (SSC) effects.

The mean values, represented by the ratio $R_{\mu}$, demonstrate remarkable consistency in capturing statistical distributions across the two simulation sets. For most summary statistics, deviations in $R_{\mu}$ are less than $1\%$, underscoring the robustness of both simulation pipelines in reproducing the underlying statistics. Moreover, comparisons with theoretical predictions (\texttt{Halofit} and \texttt{hmpdf}) reveal no significant conflicts between the theoretical models and the simulation results.

However, systematic deviations between TILED and BIGBOX simulations emerge at low $\nu$ values for peak counts, minima, and Minkowski Functionals, as well as at high $\nu$ values for minima (see the left panels in Figure~\ref{fig:nu_main}). These deviations likely result from the limited resolution of the TILED simulations in capturing super high-density and low-density regions, leading to fewer data points in the extreme tails of the distribution.

In contrast, the variance ratios $R_{\sigma^2}$ exhibit more pronounced differences, highlighting the critical impact of SSC on variance measurements. Specifically, the variance of the angular power spectrum (top-right panel in Figure~\ref{fig:ell_main}) shows a significant increase in the BIGBOX simulations at high multipole moments ($\ell$). This increase is consistent with the expected amplification due to the inclusion of larger-scale modes in the BIGBOX setup, which are absent in the TILED simulations. Furthermore, discrepancies in variance ratios grow with increasing source redshift, reflecting the progressive loss of large-scale modes in the TILED simulations. For $\nu$-binned statistics (right panels in Figure~\ref{fig:nu_main}), the BIGBOX simulations consistently yield higher variances across nearly all $\nu$ bins, with this trend becoming more pronounced at higher source redshifts. These findings emphasize the necessity of accounting for SSC and other systematic effects to ensure accurate cosmological parameter estimation from weak lensing statistics.

\section{Analysis of Covariance and Correlation Matrices}
In this section, we quantify the impact of super-sample covariance (SSC) on the covariance and correlation matrices of various summary statistics derived from the BIGBOX and TILED simulations. Figures~\ref{fig:cov_bigbox} and~\ref{fig:cov_tiled} present the covariance matrices for the BIGBOX and TILED simulations, respectively, revealing that while the general covariance structures are consistent between the two simulation sets, the BIGBOX simulations exhibit higher covariance values on average. Figure~\ref{fig:corr_main} illustrates the corresponding correlation matrices, where the upper-left triangle represents the BIGBOX results and the lower-right triangle shows the TILED results. The color scale indicates that the correlation structures are almost consistent between the two simulation sets.

Figures~\ref{fig:cov_ratio_main} and~\ref{fig:corr_ratio_main} depict the ratios of the covariance and correlation matrices between BIGBOX and TILED, denoted as $R_{\text{Cov}}$ and $R_{\rho}$, respectively. These ratios provide a quantitative assessment of SSC's influence on both the diagonal and off-diagonal elements of the covariance structure. The covariance ratios $R_{\text{Cov}}$ generally exceed unity, ranging from $10\%$ to $30\%$ above the baseline values for most summary statistics, underscoring SSC's significant contribution to the overall covariance matrix. Conversely, the correlation ratios $R_{\rho}$ typically deviate by less than $5\%$, suggesting that while SSC elevates the covariance, it has a minimal impact on the correlation coefficients. However, notable exceptions are observed in the angular power spectrum and peak/minima counts. For the angular power spectrum, larger deviations in $R_{\rho}$ are consistent with SSC effects, which introduce strong correlations through shared large-scale modes, particularly at small multipole moments ($\ell$). In the case of peak and minima counts, the deviations in correlation ratios likely arise from systematic shifts in peak positions within the TILED simulations, affecting correlations near the peak bins. For a summary of the average ratios of covariance and correlation matrices, refer to Figure~\ref{fig:avg_main}.

\section{Effects of Noise on Summary Statistics}
The presence of shape noise in convergence maps can influence the covariance and correlation structures of weak lensing summary statistics. To evaluate the interaction between shape noise and super-sample covariance (SSC) effects, we analyzed multiple noise levels corresponding to those expected in current and future surveys (see Table~\ref{tab:survey_comparison}). This analysis aims to quantify the extent to which SSC dominates over the contribution from shape noise. Since the bispectrums exhibit no clear trends in the noiseless case (as shown in Figures~\ref{fig:cov_ratio_main} and~\ref{fig:corr_ratio_main}), we focus our analysis on the angular power spectrum and select higher-order statistics. 

Figures~\ref{fig:avg_cov_noise} and~\ref{fig:cov_noise} present the covariance matrix ratios and their averages across summary statistics for the BIGBOX and TILED simulations at various noise levels. The angular power spectrum and peak/minima counts exhibit greater sensitivity to noise, particularly at high noise levels (e.g., DES and HSC surveys), with covariance ratios consistently increasing across most statistics. This trend suggests that in future surveys, SSC is expected to dominate over noise contributions. 

Conversely, Figures~\ref{fig:avg_corr_noise} and~\ref{fig:corr_noise} display the correlation matrix ratios and their averages across summary statistics at different noise levels. These ratios generally remain below $10\%$ for most statistics, indicating that noise has a minimal impact on the overall covariance structure. However, higher values in the correlation ratios are concentrated near the boundaries of each summary statistic, arising from regions where data points are sparse and measurements are more senstive to noise amplification. These findings underscore the necessity of accounting for SSC in weak lensing analyses to ensure accurate cosmological parameter estimation, especially as survey sensitivities improve and noise levels vary.

\section{Influence of Smoothing Scale}
In this section, we analyze the impact of varying Gaussian smoothing scales on higher-order summary statistics derived from convergence maps, focusing on how smoothing deviates their underlying structures. Increasing the smoothing scale progressively suppresses small-scale features, redistributing signal intensities across different scales. Notably, smoothed convergence maps were not utilized for the calculation of the angular power spectrum and bispectrum; therefore, the results presented herein correspond exclusively to other higher-order statistics.

Figures~\ref{fig:avg_cov_sl} and~\ref{fig:cov_smoothing} illustrate the changes in covariance matrix ratios and their averages as a function of the Gaussian smoothing scale. Across all smoothing scales, the average covariance ratios between the BIGBOX and TILED simulations exhibit a consistent upward trend for most summary statistics. However, notable variations occur in the peak and minima counts, particularly at larger smoothing scales. These variations are attributed to the averaging methodology, where contributions from the edges of $\nu$ bins and peak bins become increasingly significant at higher smoothing levels.

Similarly, Figures~\ref{fig:avg_corr_sl} and~\ref{fig:corr_smoothing} present the ratios of correlation matrices and their averages across different Gaussian smoothing scales. The correlation ratios generally remain below $5\%$ for most summary statistics, indicating that the overall structure of the covariance matrix is robust to variations in smoothing scale. However, consistent with the covariance trends, peak and minima counts exhibit elevated correlation ratios, especially at larger smoothing scales. These elevated ratios arise from regions with sparse data points, making measurements more susceptible to noise amplification.

In summary, these findings suggest that while Gaussian smoothing scales can influence individual summary statistics, the overall structural integrity of the covariance and correlation matrices remains largely unaffected by changes in the smoothing scale. This robustness underscores the reliability of the covariance structures in weak lensing analyses, even when different smoothing scales are applied to the convergence maps.

\section{Systematic Effects: Box Replication Artifact} \label{sec:boxreplication}
In weak lensing simulations, extending the simulated volume often necessitates replicating finite simulation boxes to create a larger effective volume. This box replication process can introduce artificial correlations in the data, particularly near the boundaries or along preferred replication directions. Such artifacts may compromise the reliability of higher-order summary statistics. To systematically investigate this issue, we categorize simulation regions into two distinct groups: regions significantly affected by artifacts, termed \textbf{Replication-Influenced Patches (RIP)}, and regions minimally affected by artifacts, termed \textbf{Replication-Minimal Patches (RMP)}.

The criteria for categorizing patches are as follows:
\begin{itemize} 
    \item \textbf{Replication-Influenced Patches (RIP)}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Patches near the equator}: 
        \[
            \left| \theta_i - \frac{\pi}{2} \right| \leq R_{\text{patch}}
        \]
        \item \textit{Patches near the edges of octants}: 
        \[
            \left| \phi_i - \frac{k\pi}{2} \right| \leq R_{\text{patch}} \quad \text{for} \quad k = 0, 1, 2, 3
        \]
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Replication-Minimal Patches (RMP)}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item All other patches not meeting the above criteria.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
where $(\theta_i, \phi_i)$ denote the center of patch $i$, and $R_{\text{patch}} = 5\sqrt{2}\, \mathrm{deg}$ is the half-diagonal of the patch.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/results/patch_replication_heavily.png}
    \caption[Angular Power Spectrum for Heavily Influenced RIPs]
    {
        Angular power spectrum for the Replication-Influenced Patches (RIPs) heavily influenced by box replication artifacts. The systematic biases introduced by these artifacts are evident in the deviations from the values observed in other patches.
    }
    \label{fig:boxreplication_heavy}
\end{figure}

Our analysis indicates that patches containing the point $(\theta_i, \phi_i) = (\pi/2, 0)$ exhibit significant deviations in both the mean and variance of summary statistics compared to other RIPs. To prevent these outliers from skewing our results, we exclude this specific point from subsequent analyses. Figure~\ref{fig:boxreplication_heavy} presents the angular power spectrum for the heavily influenced RIPs, highlighting the systematic biases introduced by box replication artifacts.

After these adjustments, we obtained $70$ RMPs per realization, resulting in a total of $1,!400$ RMPs for the TILED simulations and $770$ RMPs for the BIGBOX simulations across all realizations.

Figure~\ref{fig:boxreplication_main} presents the ratios of mean and variance for various summary statistics between Replication-Influenced Patches (RIPs) and Replication-Minimal Patches (RMPs). The analysis reveals that the mean angular power spectra in RIPs are systematically underestimated by approximately $0.5\%$. Additionally, the $\nu$-binned statistics exhibit elevated mean values in low $\nu$ bins and reduced values in high $\nu$ bins. This pattern suggests that box replication intensifies both overdense and underdense regions, leading to more extreme convergence values. Specifically, in the TILED simulations, the limited resolution in underdense regions causes a shift of the $\nu$-binned statistics toward lower $\nu$ bins. Conversely, the variance ratios approach unity, indicating that the box replication effect amplifies variance to levels comparable to those observed in the BIGBOX simulations. Notably, the dependence on source redshift is effectively nullified in RIPs, implying that box replication obscures the super-sample variance.
