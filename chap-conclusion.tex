In this study, we performed a set of simulations: TILED and BIGBOX, to investigate the impacts of super-sample effect on the covariance and correlation matrices of the power spectrum and higher-order statistics. We utilized 11 realizations of state-of-the-art simulations, using $6144^3$ particles within $3750\ \mathrm{Mpc}/h$ from the HalfDome project \citep{2024arXiv240717462B} as BIGBOX, and accordingly computed 20 realizations of the same volume tiled with smaller boxes with the same cosmology, where each box component uses $1024^3$ particles within $625\ \mathrm{Mpc}/h$, as TILED. We considered the convergence field propagated under the Born approximation. We extracted $10^\circ \times 10^\circ$ patches from full-sky convergence maps via a Fibonacci grid, achieving uniform coverage with minimal overlap.

To probe non-Gaussian features of the convergence field, we employ a suite of higher-order statistics, including the angular power spectrum, bispectrum, probability distribution function (PDF), peak and minima counts, and Minkowski functionals. These measures are computed using both established tools and custom implementations, with validation against theoretical models such as \texttt{Halofit} and \texttt{hmpdf}. By examining a wide range of scales, from $\ell$-binned statistics ($\ell \in [300, 3000]$) to $\nu$-binned statistics ($-4 \leq \kappa/\sigma_\kappa \leq 4$), we capture the broad impact of the super-sample effect across different observational regimes.

Below, we summarize our key findings and their implications, based on a comparative analysis of statistical measures and their covariance and correlation matrices in BIGBOX and TILED simulations:
\begin{itemize} 
    \item Comparing mean values of statistical measures between BIGBOX and TILED simulations revealed consistent results within $1\%$ across all statistics, redshifts, and multipoles. This agreement underscores the reliability of the simulations in capturing the underlying cosmological model. 
    \item Despite similarity in mean values, variance ratios differ by $10\%$-$30\%$, with the largest deviations occurring at high multipoles and redshifts. These discrepancies arise from the super-sample effect, which is driven by large-scale modes present in BIGBOX but absent in TILED.
    \item shape noise notably affects certain observables, particularly the angular power spectrum and peak/minima counts, with deviations becoming more pronounced at higher noise levels. Nonetheless, the super-sample effect remains dominant, and correlation matrices exhibit minimal structural changes ($<5\%$), indicating robust covariance estimation even in the presence of noise.
    \item Varying the Gaussian smoothing scale primarily modifies small-scale map features without substantially altering covariance or correlation matrices. Higher-order statistics also show only modest changes, supporting the notion that the super-sample effect is largely independent of smoothing scales.
    \item Box replication artifacts introduce systematic challenges, leading to $\sim 2\%$-$5\%$ biases in mean summary statistics within Replication-Influenced Patches (RIP). Covariance and correlation ratios in these patches deviate by up to $10\%$ relative to BIGBOX, indicating higher covariance levels and potentially obscuring redshift-dependent trends associated with the super-sample variance. These findings emphasize the need to mitigate box replication artifacts for unbiased simulation-based analyses.
\end{itemize}

\at{These results have significant implications for weak lensing surveys such as LSST, Euclid, and Roman. The substantial influence of super-sample variance underscores the importance of including large-scale modes in simulations to ensure accurate variance modeling. The demonstrated robustness of the super-sample effect to shape noise and smoothing scales further reinforces its relevance across diverse observational settings. However, mitigating systematic effects, particularly box replication artifacts, remains critical to prevent biased interpretations of higher-order statistics.}

\at{A dimensional analysis suggests that $R_{\sigma^2} \propto \left(L^2 / \Omega_W\right)^\alpha$, where $L$ is the simulation box size, $\Omega_W$ is the survey area, and $\alpha$ is an undetermined exponent. Accordingly, super-sample variance becomes more pronounced for larger surveys when box sizes are relatively small. For instance, in a survey with an area of $18{,}000\ \mathrm{\deg}^2$ (e.g., LSST), achieving super-sample variance levels comparable to TILED would require a simulation box size of $625\ \mathrm{Mpc}/h \times \sqrt{18{,}000/100} \approx 8\ \mathrm{Gpc}/h$.}

Future work will focus on the following aspects:
\begin{itemize} 
    \item Developing methods to mitigate box replication artifacts, especially in simulations utilizing simply replicated volumes. 
    \item Extending the analysis to incorporate additional cosmological models and baryonic effects to enhance simulation realism. 
    \item Integrating these insights into survey design and data analysis pipelines to improve the precision of cosmological parameter inference. 
\end{itemize}

By refining simulation techniques and observational approaches, weak lensing remains a powerful method for probing fundamental cosmic properties and constraining cosmological models with increasingly stringent accuracy.
